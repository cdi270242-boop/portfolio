01

Flagship project — E-commerce Customer Analytics

One end-to-end project, worked the way my Project Page lays it out: a data ingestion pipeline, a MySQL database design, a full analysis, and three report dashboards. An ecommerce business can’t tell which customers, categories, or months actually make money — so I built the dataset in MySQL (25 customers, 46 orders), cleaned and queried it in SQL, and reported it as three dashboards: customer segmentation, an estimated P&L, and a products report. Every figure in this analysis is from that full 25-customer dataset.

Q2

Which product categories drive the most revenue, and at what average order value?

Q3

Q4

How does monthly revenue turn into profit — and which assumptions sit behind that view?

Findings → recommendations

FindingEvidenceRecommendation
Value is highly concentrated VIPs are 4 of 25 customers (16%) but hold $8,855.50 of the $17,504.49 total lifetime value — 50.6%. VIP + Loyal together hold 77.8%. Protect the top: retention attention on 10 customers covers over three-quarters of lifetime value, and any VIP going quiet should be flagged early.
Electronics carries the revenue $3,291.99 of $7,433.47 order revenue (44.3%) from 12 orders, at a $274.33 average order value — more than double the other categories (~$118–$125). Electronics is both the strength and the risk: watch its monthly trend separately, and use Clothing ($1,297.50) and Groceries ($1,238.00) to broaden the base.
The average VIP is worth 8× the rest Average lifetime value: VIP $2,213.88 per customer, Loyal $793.33, all other segments $259.27. Moving one Loyal customer up a segment is worth more than acquiring several new ones — prioritise repeat-purchase follow-up for the Loyal group.
Profit is an estimate, not a fact Monthly revenue grew from under $100 a month in 2023 to a $2,541.50 peak in Oct 2024. The P&L applies assumed costs — 60% COGS, 15% operating costs — giving an estimated 40% gross margin and 25% net margin. Use the P&L for trend and mix, and replace the assumed cost rates with real ones before making pricing or budget decisions.

03

E-Commerce Database Design

The second page of the project: a four-table MySQL schema that turns the loaded extracts into a customer-analysis database. customer_segments holds the segment definitions, customer_profiles one row per customer with lifetime value, purchase_history the 46 orders, and segment_events an audit log of every move between segments.

On GitHub it is two SQL files: customer_segmentation_setup.sql ↗ creates the schema and segment rules, and customer_segmentation_additional_rows.sql ↗ adds the remaining customer rows that bring the base to the full 25 customers.

Segment rules (from the SQL schema)

A customer lands in the highest segment whose thresholds they clear; recency rules catch the rest.

SegmentMin ordersMin spendMeaning
VIP10$1,000High spend, high frequency
Loyal5$500Consistent, moderate spend
Occasional2$100Infrequent, moderate spend
New1$0First-time / very recent
At Risk——Previously active, gone quiet
Churned——No purchase in 180+ days

A segment_events audit table logs every move between segments, so migration can be tracked over time.

The kind of SQL behind it

Segment summary query from the segmentation schema:

SELECT cs.segment_name, COUNT(cp.profile_id) AS customer_count, ROUND(AVG(cp.lifetime_value), 2) AS avg_ltv, ROUND(SUM(cp.lifetime_value), 2) AS total_lifetime_value FROM customer_segments cs LEFT JOIN customer_profiles cp ON cs.segment_id = cp.segment_id GROUP BY cs.segment_name ORDER BY total_lifetime_value DESC;

Note the label: the query sums lifetime_value, so it is reported as total lifetime value — order revenue ($7,433.47) is measured separately from the orders table. The schema also ships a re-segmentation UPDATE that re-assigns every customer against the thresholds above.
